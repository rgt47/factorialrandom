\author{Ronald G. Thomas, Ph.D.\thanks{Wertheim School of Public Health, UCSD; ORCID: 0000-0003-1686-4965}}

\begin{abstract}
Factorial trials test several treatments at once. In
moderate samples, simple randomization often leaves the
treatment combinations unbalanced on baseline covariates.
Matched-tuple randomization groups subjects into sets of \(k\)
similar subjects, one per treatment combination. The
combinations are then randomized within each set. This
paper studies the design for trials drawn from a
pre-characterized pool, such as an Alzheimer's disease
trial-ready cohort. We give a greedy tuple-forming heuristic
and a pool-oversampling variant. The contrast estimator is
unbiased, and a between-tuple variance estimator is
conservative. Formal asymptotic theory for this design is
due to Bai, Liu and Tabord-Meehan (2024). A simulation
compared five designs in 72 scenarios with four arms.
Matched tuples improved balance and efficiency over simple
randomization. Their efficiency was similar to that of
rerandomization with a calibrated threshold.
Regression adjustment recovered most of the gain under every
design. Pool oversampling improved balance. It also excluded
subjects with unusual covariate values. This changes the
estimand to the effect among retained subjects. We provide
the R package \texttt{factorialrandom}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{The design problem}\label{the-design-problem}

Clinical trials increasingly evaluate combinations of
treatments. Three examples are anti-amyloid plus anti-tau
therapies in Alzheimer's disease, drug plus behavioral
intervention in oncology supportive care, and
multi-component public health programs. Each gives rise to
a factorial treatment structure. A \(2 \times 2\) factorial
design assigns subjects to one of four combinations. These
are neither factor, factor A alone, factor B alone, and
both. Under the hidden replication principle\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fisherDesignExperiments1935}{1}}, each subject contributes to
the estimation of both main effects and their interaction.
That is, absent interaction, each main effect is estimated
with the precision of a two-arm trial that uses all \(N\)
subjects.

The central challenge is covariate balance. With four or
more treatment arms, simple randomization can produce
substantial imbalance on prognostic covariates. This
imbalance inflates variance and threatens the credibility of
treatment comparisons. The problem is greater in moderate
sample sizes, for example a total of \(N = 100\) to \(500\)
subjects, or 25 to 125 per arm in a \(2 \times 2\) design.
Sample sizes of this order are typical of Phase II and
prevention trials.

\subsection{Covariate balance strategies}\label{covariate-balance-strategies}

Existing approaches to covariate balance were developed
primarily for two-arm trials. These include stratified
randomization\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kernanStratifiedRandomization1999}{2}},
minimization\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-pocockSequentialTreatment1975}{3}}, and
rerandomization\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morganRerandomization2012}{4}}. Stratification
is limited to a few categorical covariates. Minimization
extends to multiple covariates, but it operates sequentially
as subjects enroll. This sequential operation is unnecessary
when the full pool is available at design time.
Rerandomization accepts only allocations that meet a balance
criterion.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bransonRerandomization2KFactorial2016}{5}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liRerandomization2KFactorial2020}{6}} extended it to \(2^K\)
factorial designs.

Matched-pair randomization is the most aggressive form of
restricted randomization.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-baiOptimalityMatchedPairDesigns2022}{7}} proved that, among all
stratified designs with equal allocation probabilities, a
specific matched-pair design achieves minimum asymptotic
variance.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-imaiVarianceIdentification2008}{8}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fogartyRegressionAssisted2018}{9}},\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fogartyFinelyStratified2018}{10}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-baiMatchedPairsInference2022}{11}} developed the inferential
framework for pairs. The extension beyond pairs also exists.
1 study estimation and inference for
matched tuples. Their setting explicitly includes `fully
blocked' \(2^K\) factorial designs. This is the design we
study here.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-higginsThresholdBlocking2016}{12}} give a scalable
blocking algorithm for many arms with an approximation
guarantee.

\subsection{Trial-ready cohorts}\label{trial-ready-cohorts}

The trial-ready cohort (TRC) paradigm was developed for
Alzheimer's disease prevention research. It pre-screens and
characterizes large pools of potential trial participants.
The TRC-PAD project\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-aisenTRCPADOverview2020}{13},\citeproc{ref-aisenTRCPADExperience2020}{14}} enrolled over 30,000 individuals
in its web-based screening component. It further
characterized about 2,000 in-person participants with
cognitive testing, APOE genotyping, and amyloid biomarkers.
The AHEAD 3-45 study\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rafiiAHEAD2023}{15}} and the DIAN-TU
platform\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-batemanDIAN2012}{16},\citeproc{ref-bateman2017DIANTU}{17}} draw on
similar infrastructure.

These characterized pools create an opportunity. When the full
cohort is available before randomization, multivariate
matching can be performed optimally rather than sequentially.
The detailed baseline phenotyping (amyloid PET, tau PET,
cognitive composites, genetics) provides strong prognostic
covariates. We note that the factorial treatment structures
now emerging in combination therapy trials create a natural
demand for \(k > 2\) matching.

\subsection{Contribution}\label{contribution}

We propose matched-tuple randomization for factorial designs.
Subjects are grouped into tuples of size \(k\) based on
multivariate baseline covariates. Here \(k\) is the number of
treatment combinations. Treatment assignment is then
constrained. The constraint is that each tuple member
receives a distinct combination. The design itself is not
new. 1 give its asymptotic theory. Our
contribution is applied, and it has four parts.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  A greedy heuristic for forming matched \(k\)-tuples, and a
  pool-oversampling variant. The variant forms tuples from a
  larger characterized pool and keeps the tightest ones. We
  state the estimand this variant targets.
\item
  Short finite-population arguments for unbiasedness of the
  contrast estimator and for conservativeness of a
  between-tuple variance estimator. We do not derive
  asymptotic distributions; for these we refer to

  \begin{enumerate}
  \def\labelenumii{\arabic{enumii}.}
  \tightlist
  \item
  \end{enumerate}
\item
  A simulation study against simple, stratified and
  rerandomized designs, with and without regression
  adjustment. The scenarios are motivated by Alzheimer's
  disease prevention trials.
\item
  An R package implementing the methods.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}\label{background}

\subsection{Factorial designs}\label{factorial-designs}

\subsubsection{Classical framework}\label{classical-framework}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fisherDesignExperiments1935}{1}} introduced factorial experiments
to evaluate multiple factors simultaneously. Consider \(F\)
factors, each at two levels (\(+\) and \(-\)). The \(2^F\) factorial
assigns subjects to all level combinations. Main effects and
interactions are defined as signed averages of cell means. The
main efficiency result is straightforward. Every observation
contributes to every estimable effect.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-dasguptaCausalInference2015}{18}} recast factorial designs within
the potential outcomes framework. For a \(2^2\) factorial, each
subject has four potential outcomes,
\(Y_i(00), Y_i(10), Y_i(01), Y_i(11)\), corresponding to the
four treatment combinations. The main effect of factor A is
defined as follows.
\begin{equation}
  \tau_A = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left[ \frac{Y_i(10) + Y_i(11)}{2} -
           \frac{Y_i(00) + Y_i(01)}{2} \right]
\end{equation}
Factor B and the interaction \(AB\) are defined analogously.

\subsubsection{Factorial designs in clinical trials}\label{factorial-designs-in-clinical-trials}

Factorial designs are increasingly used in clinical trials
to evaluate combination therapies and multi-component
interventions.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-collinsMultiphaseOptimization2007}{19}} introduced
the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST). MOST uses
factorial experiments to identify active intervention
components.

In Alzheimer's disease, combination therapies appear in the
current drug development pipeline\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-cummingsADTrialDesign2024}{20}}. Plausible future prevention
trials could combine mechanisms of action. Examples are
anti-amyloid antibodies with anti-tau agents, or a drug with
a lifestyle intervention. Such combinations create natural
factorial structures. These examples are ours. They are not
drawn from the cited pipeline report.

\subsection{Matched randomization}\label{matched-randomization}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Matched-pair designs (\(k = 2\))}{Matched-pair designs (k = 2)}}\label{matched-pair-designs-k-2}

In a matched-pair design, \(N\) subjects are grouped into \(M =
N/2\) pairs based on baseline covariates. Within each pair, one
subject is randomized to treatment and the other to control.
The treatment effect estimator is given below.
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\tau} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    (Y_{m,T} - Y_{m,C})
\end{equation}
where \(Y_{m,T}\) and \(Y_{m,C}\) are the outcomes for the treated
and control subjects in pair \(m\).

Label the two units in pair \(m\) as \(1\) and \(2\). Write
\(T_m = Y_{m,T} - Y_{m,C}\) for the pair difference. Under
within-pair randomization, \(T_m\) equals \(Y_{m1}(1) -
Y_{m2}(0)\) or \(Y_{m2}(1) - Y_{m1}(0)\), each with probability
\(1/2\), and pairs are independent. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \text{Var}(\hat{\tau}) = \frac{1}{4M^2} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \left\{ [Y_{m1}(1) + Y_{m1}(0)] -
            [Y_{m2}(1) + Y_{m2}(0)] \right\}^2 ,
\end{equation}
by direct calculation (see also\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-imaiVarianceIdentification2008}{8}}). The variance is small when
the two members of each pair have similar potential
outcomes. Matching on prognostic covariates aims at exactly
this. The usual estimator \(s_T^2/M\), with \(s_T^2\) the sample
variance of the \(T_m\), is conservative. Its bias is
\(\sum_m (\tau_m - \bar\tau)^2 / \{M(M-1)\}\), where \(\tau_m\)
is the average treatment effect of the two units in pair
\(m\).

\subsubsection{Optimal matching}\label{optimal-matching}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-greevyOptimalMatching2004}{21}} formed pairs before
randomization by optimal nonbipartite matching. A single
pool is not split into two groups in advance, so the graph
is not bipartite. Minimum-weight perfect matching on a
general graph is solvable in \(O(N^3)\) time. The distance
between subjects \(i\) and \(j\) is
typically the Mahalanobis distance, defined below.
\begin{equation}
  d(i,j) = \sqrt{(X_i - X_j)' S^{-1} (X_i - X_j)}
\end{equation}
where \(X_i\) is the covariate vector and \(S\) is the sample
covariance matrix.

\subsubsection{Rerandomization}\label{rerandomization}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morganRerandomization2012}{4}} proposed rerandomization as an
intermediate approach between simple randomization and
deterministic matching. A randomization is accepted only if
the Mahalanobis distance between treatment group covariate
means falls below a threshold \(a\):
\begin{equation}
  M = (\bar{X}_T - \bar{X}_C)' \hat{\text{cov}}(\bar{X}_T -
    \bar{X}_C)^{-1} (\bar{X}_T - \bar{X}_C) \le a
\end{equation}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liRerandomizationAsymptotics2018}{22}} derived the asymptotic
distribution of treatment effect estimators under
rerandomization. It is a convolution of a normal and a
truncated normal, with smaller variance than under complete
randomization.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bransonRerandomization2KFactorial2016}{5}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liRerandomization2KFactorial2020}{6}} developed rerandomization
for \(2^K\) factorial designs.

\subsection{Trial-ready cohorts in Alzheimer's disease}\label{trial-ready-cohorts-in-alzheimers-disease}

\subsubsection{The recruitment challenge}\label{the-recruitment-challenge}

Preclinical Alzheimer's disease is defined by biomarker
evidence of brain amyloid pathology\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sperlingTrialDesignAD2011}{23}}. Prevention trials therefore
confirm amyloid status by PET imaging or cerebrospinal
fluid analysis. Many volunteers screen out at this step,
and each screen is costly. These
constraints motivate the trial-ready cohort approach. In this
approach, screening and characterization are performed
prospectively. The result is a pool of eligible participants
ready for rapid enrollment.

\subsubsection{TRC-PAD}\label{trc-pad}

The Trial-Ready Cohort for Preclinical/Prodromal Alzheimer's
Disease (TRC-PAD) was established by the Alzheimer's Clinical
Trials Consortium\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-aisenTRCPADOverview2020}{13}}. The architecture
includes:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  An online longitudinal screening study (APT Webstudy) with
  quarterly cognitive assessments. Over 30,000 individuals
  consented by 2020.
\item
  Machine learning risk prediction using demographic, genetic,
  and cognitive data to identify individuals likely to have
  elevated brain amyloid.
\item
  In-person screening with cognitive testing and biomarker
  confirmation. Remote collection of blood for plasma
  biomarkers has been piloted for preclinical AD trials\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-walterAlzMatch2024}{24}}.
\item
  Longitudinal follow-up of biomarker-confirmed eligible
  participants until a matching trial opens.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{AHEAD 3-45}\label{ahead-3-45}

The AHEAD 3-45 study\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rafiiAHEAD2023}{15}} tests lecanemab in
preclinical AD using a sister-trial design. This design pairs
A3 (intermediate amyloid, Phase 2, imaging endpoints) with A45
(elevated amyloid, Phase 3, cognitive endpoints). Both trials
share a common screening process, drawing from TRC-PAD and
direct recruitment. The study shows how TRC infrastructure
feeds directly into clinical trials.

\subsubsection{DIAN-TU and EPAD}\label{dian-tu-and-epad}

The Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-batemanDIAN2012}{16}} maintains a registry of autosomal dominant
AD mutation carriers. This registry provides a genetically
defined trial-ready cohort. The DIAN-TU platform\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bateman2017DIANTU}{17}} implemented adaptive designs with shared
placebo arms and cognitive run-in periods.

The European Prevention of Alzheimer's Dementia (EPAD)
program\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-solomonEPAD2022}{25}} recruited over 2,000 participants
into a longitudinal cohort across 39 European institutions.
It was explicitly designed as a readiness cohort for adaptive
proof-of-concept trials.

\subsubsection{Relevance to matched factorial designs}\label{relevance-to-matched-factorial-designs}

Trial-ready cohorts provide the prerequisites for
matched-tuple randomization. These prerequisites are large
characterized pools with detailed baseline phenotyping. The
pool-to-sample ratio, defined as available subjects divided
by trial enrollment target, determines matching quality.
TRC-PAD has a pool of thousands of characterized individuals.
Combination therapy trials are producing factorial treatment
structures. Together, these create what we regard as an ideal
application setting.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Notation}\label{notation}

Consider \(N\) subjects to be randomized across a \(2^F\)
factorial design with \(k = 2^F\) treatment combinations.
Subjects are grouped into \(M = N/k\) matched tuples. Each
tuple contains \(k\) subjects with similar baseline covariate
profiles. Within each tuple, the \(k\) treatment combinations
are assigned uniformly at random to the \(k\) subjects.

Let \(X_i \in \mathbb{R}^p\) denote the baseline covariate
vector for subject \(i\). Let \(Y_i(z)\) denote the potential
outcome for subject \(i\) under treatment combination \(z \in
\{0, 1\}^F\). The observed outcome is \(Y_i = Y_i(Z_i)\) where
\(Z_i\) is the assigned treatment.

\subsection{Matching algorithm}\label{matching-algorithm}

\subsubsection{Distance metric}\label{distance-metric}

We consider two distance metrics for tuple formation:

\textbf{Mahalanobis distance.} For subjects \(i\) and \(j\):
\begin{equation}
  d_M(i,j) = \sqrt{(X_i - X_j)' S^{-1} (X_i - X_j)}
\end{equation}
where \(S\) is the pooled sample covariance matrix of the
covariates.

\textbf{Prognostic score distance.} When historical data or pilot
study results are available, we fit a model \(\hat{m}(X) =
E[Y(0) \mid X]\) predicting the outcome under the reference
condition. The prognostic score distance is then defined as
follows.
\begin{equation}
  d_P(i,j) = |\hat{m}(X_i) - \hat{m}(X_j)|
\end{equation}
Matching on the prognostic score concentrates precision gains
on the most relevant covariate dimension.

\subsubsection{Tuple formation}\label{tuple-formation}

Given \(N\) subjects and a tuple size \(k\), we partition the
subjects into \(M = \lfloor N/k \rfloor\) tuples. The aim is to
minimize total within-tuple dispersion, as follows.
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathcal{P}} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \sum_{i < j \in \mathcal{T}_m} d(i,j)
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{P} = \{\mathcal{T}_1, \ldots,
\mathcal{T}_M\}\) is a partition into tuples.

For \(k = 2\), this reduces to optimal pair matching. Optimal
pair matching is solvable in polynomial time. For \(k > 2\),
we use a heuristic.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-higginsThresholdBlocking2016}{12}} give an approximation
algorithm with a guarantee for a closely related problem,
in which blocks have at least \(k\) units. The
\texttt{factorialrandom} package currently implements one
heuristic, described below.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Sequential greedy.} Select a seed subject, find the
  \(k-1\) nearest neighbors, form a tuple, remove from pool,
  repeat. Seeds are ordered by their mean distance to all
  other subjects, largest first, to avoid stranding
  outliers. Implemented as
  \texttt{match\_tuples(...,\ method\ =\ "greedy")}.
\end{enumerate}

Hierarchical clustering and integer-programming formulations
of the same partition problem are natural extensions.
Neither is implemented. The option
\texttt{match\_tuples(...,\ method\ =\ "optimal")} currently aliases to
the greedy heuristic. All results below use the greedy
heuristic only. We have not measured how far it falls short
of an optimal partition.

\subsubsection{Pool oversampling}\label{pool-oversampling}

When the trial-ready cohort provides \(N_{\text{pool}} > N\)
characterized subjects, matching quality improves. The
algorithm selects the \(N\) subjects that form the best \(M\)
tuples, leaving \(N_{\text{pool}} - N\) subjects unmatched. The
pool-to-sample ratio \(r = N_{\text{pool}} / N\) is a design
parameter. A larger \(r\) yields tighter within-tuple matching.
The cost is that more subjects are excluded.

The exclusion is not random. The trimming step keeps the
tuples with the smallest mean within-tuple distance. These
tuples lie in dense regions of covariate space. Subjects in
sparse regions are dropped more often. In AD these could be
the oldest participants, those with extreme amyloid levels,
or carriers of rare genotypes. The randomization is still
valid for the retained subjects. The estimand, however,
changes. With \(r > 1\) the design estimates the average
effect among the retained \(N\) subjects, not among the
\(N_{\text{pool}}\) characterized subjects. Under a constant
treatment effect the two coincide. Under effect
heterogeneity related to the covariates they can differ.
Section \ref{pool-selection} quantifies this for an
illustrative effect modifier. Excluding sparse-region
subjects also raises eligibility and fairness questions in
a trial-ready cohort. A sponsor using \(r > 1\) should state
the retained-sample estimand in the protocol and report how
retained and excluded subjects differ.

\subsection{Randomization distribution}\label{randomization-distribution}

Within each tuple \(m\), the \(k\) treatment combinations are
assigned to the \(k\) subjects by drawing uniformly from the
\(k!\) permutations. Tuples are independent. The randomization
space therefore has \((k!)^M\) equally likely allocations.

This constrained randomization guarantees that for every
tuple, each treatment arm contains exactly one subject.
Consequently, all factorial contrasts (main effects,
interactions) are perfectly balanced within each tuple.

\subsection{Estimation}\label{estimation}

\subsubsection{Difference-in-means}\label{difference-in-means}

For a factorial contrast \(c\) defined by a coefficient vector
\(w_z\) over treatment combinations (with \(\sum_z w_z = 0\)),
the estimator is given below.
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\tau}_c = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \sum_{z} w_z \, Y_{m}(z)
\end{equation}
where \(Y_{m}(z)\) is the outcome of the subject in tuple \(m\)
assigned to treatment \(z\). For a \(2^F\) design we use
\(w_z = \pm 1/2^{F-1}\). This matches the definition of
\(\tau_A\) in equation (1).

Write \(T_m(c) = \sum_z w_z Y_m(z)\) for the tuple-level
contrast, so \(\hat\tau_c = M^{-1} \sum_m T_m(c)\). The
estimator is unbiased for the average factorial effect of
the \(N\) matched subjects. The proof is short. Within tuple
\(m\), the uniform permutation gives each member probability
\(1/k\) of receiving each \(z\). Hence \(E[Y_m(z)] =
\bar{Y}_m(z)\), the mean of \(Y_i(z)\) over the \(k\) members.
So \(E[T_m(c)] = \sum_z w_z \bar{Y}_m(z) = \tau_{c,m}\), the
average effect in tuple \(m\). Averaging over tuples gives
\(E[\hat\tau_c] = M^{-1}\sum_m \tau_{c,m} = \tau_c\).

\subsubsection{Variance estimation}\label{variance-estimation}

Tuples are randomized independently. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \text{Var}(\hat{\tau}_c) = \frac{1}{M^2}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M} V_m(c), \qquad
  V_m(c) = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\pi}
    \left[ T_m^{\pi}(c) - \tau_{c,m} \right]^2 ,
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the \(k!\) assignments \(\pi\) of the
\(k\) combinations to the tuple members. \(T_m^{\pi}(c)\) is the
contrast that assignment \(\pi\) would produce. \(V_m(c)\) is
small when the members of tuple \(m\) have similar potential
outcomes.

The package offers two estimators of this variance. The
paired estimator is \(s_T^2/M\), where \(s_T^2\) is the sample
variance of the \(M\) tuple contrasts \(T_m(c)\) (between
tuples, not within). The \(T_m(c)\) are independent with
means \(\tau_{c,m}\). The same algebra as for pairs then gives
\begin{equation}
  E\left[ s_T^2 / M \right] = \text{Var}(\hat{\tau}_c) +
    \frac{1}{M(M-1)} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    (\tau_{c,m} - \tau_c)^2 .
\end{equation}
The estimator is therefore conservative. It is unbiased
when the tuple effects are constant. It has \(M - 1\) degrees
of freedom, so the package forms intervals and tests with
\(t_{M-1}\) critical values. The second is the unpaired
Neyman estimator \(\sum_z w_z^2 s_z^2 / n_z\), which ignores
the tuples. It is used with normal critical values. We do
not prove its conservativeness under matched-tuple
randomization. Its empirical coverage is reported below.
1 construct a consistent estimator of
the asymptotic variance for matched tuples. The package
does not implement it.

\subsubsection{Regression adjustment}\label{regression-adjustment}

Following\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linAgnosticNotes2013}{26}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fogartyRegressionAssisted2018}{9}}, we augment the
difference-in-means with a regression adjustment:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\tau}_c^{\text{adj}} = \hat{\tau}_c -
    \hat{\beta}'(\bar{X}_c^{+} - \bar{X}_c^{-})
\end{equation}
where \(\hat{\beta}\) is estimated from an outcome regression
and \(\bar{X}_c^{+}, \bar{X}_c^{-}\) are the covariate means
for the positive and negative arms of contrast \(c\). The
package implements the fully interacted form of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linAgnosticNotes2013}{26}}. It regresses \(Y\) on the \(k\) arm
indicators and their interactions with centered covariates,
and takes \(\hat\tau_c^{\text{adj}} = \sum_z w_z
\hat\alpha_z\) from the arm intercepts \(\hat\alpha_z\). The
standard error is the HC2 sandwich estimate of
\(\text{Var}(\sum_z w_z \hat\alpha_z)\), with normal critical
values. This variance ignores the tuple structure. The
model fits \(k(p+1)\) coefficients, 36 when \(p = 8\) and
\(k = 4\). We apply the same adjustment to the unmatched
designs. Lin-adjusted complete randomization is the usual
default in practice, so it is the relevant benchmark. Under
matched-tuple randomization \(\bar{X}_c^{+} - \bar{X}_c^{-}\)
is already close to zero, so the adjustment is expected to
change the estimate little.

\subsection{Efficiency analysis}\label{efficiency-analysis}

\subsubsection{Relative efficiency}\label{relative-efficiency}

Define the relative efficiency of matched-tuple randomization
versus simple randomization as:
\begin{equation}
  \text{RE} = \frac{\text{Var}_{\text{simple}}(\hat{\tau}_c)}
    {\text{Var}_{\text{matched}}(\hat{\tau}_c)}
\end{equation}

We give a heuristic bound for the linear outcome model of
the simulation (Section \ref{data-generating-mechanism}).
It is not a formal result.

Let \(\sigma^2\) be the variance of \(Y(z)\) within an arm, and
let \(R^2\) be the share explained by \(X\). Each contrast has
weights \(\pm 2/k\) over \(k\) arms of \(N/k\) subjects. Under
simple randomization the variance of \(\hat\tau_c\) is then
about \(4\sigma^2/N\). Suppose tuples were perfectly matched,
with identical \(X\) within each tuple. The \(X'\beta\) term
would then cancel in every \(T_m(c)\). Only the residual
variance \(\sigma^2(1-R^2)\) would remain, giving about
\(4\sigma^2(1-R^2)/N\). Hence, in this model,
\begin{equation}
  \text{RE} \le \frac{1}{1 - R^2} ,
\end{equation}
approximately, and the bound does not depend on \(M\).
Imperfect matching gives less. The same bound is the
large-sample efficiency of Lin's adjusted estimator under
complete randomization in a linear model. Under linearity,
then, matching and regression adjustment target the same
gain. Matching may still help in small samples, with many
covariates, or with nonlinear outcome models. The
simulation reports empirical RE against this bound
(Table \ref{tab:re-table}). 1 give the
formal asymptotic comparison.

\subsubsection{Degrees of freedom}\label{degrees-of-freedom}

Under simple randomization with \(N\) subjects and \(k\) arms,
the residual degrees of freedom are \(N - k\). The paired
variance estimator for matched tuples has \(M - 1 = N/k - 1\)
degrees of freedom. With small \(M\) this loss widens
intervals and can offset the precision gain from matching\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-martinMatchingPower1993}{27}}. The package therefore uses
\(t_{M-1}\) critical values for the paired estimator. The
simulation reports its coverage at \(M = 25\)
(\(N = 100\), \(k = 4\)). We do not propose a threshold for \(M\).

\subsection{Design}\label{design}

We evaluate five randomization strategies in a \(2 \times 2\)
factorial design (\(k = 4\)), under scenarios motivated by
Alzheimer's disease prevention trials.

Subjects have \(p\) baseline covariates drawn from a
multivariate normal distribution with AR(1) correlation
(\(\rho = 0.3\)). The outcome is generated according to the
following model.
\begin{equation}
  Y_i = X_i' \beta + \frac{\tau_A}{2} a_i +
    \frac{\tau_B}{2} b_i + \frac{\tau_{AB}}{2} a_i b_i +
    \varepsilon_i
\end{equation}
Here \(a_i, b_i \in \{-1, +1\}\) code the two factors. With
this coding, \(\tau_A\), \(\tau_B\) and \(\tau_{AB}\) are the
factorial effects defined by the weights \(\pm 1/2\), as in
equation (1). An indicator-coded model with an \(A_i B_i\)
term would have coefficient \(2\tau_{AB}\) on that term. The
weights \(\beta\) are equal across covariates. They are
scaled so that \(\text{Var}(X'\beta) = R^2\) under the AR(1)
covariance, and \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1 - R^2)\). The
outcome variance within an arm is then 1, and \(R^2\) is the
population share explained by \(X\). The effects are constant
across subjects. Main effects are in SD units.

\subsubsection{Comparator methods}\label{comparator-methods}

The stratified design uses one covariate, \(X_1\). The
covariates have equal weights in the outcome model, so no
covariate is more prognostic than another. Stratifying on
one of \(p\) covariates is therefore a weak comparator. We
keep it because single-factor stratification is common in
practice.

For rerandomization, \(M_k = \sum_z n_z (\bar{X}_z -
\bar{X})' S^{-1} (\bar{X}_z - \bar{X})\), where \(S\) is the
sample covariance of the individual covariates. Since
\(\text{Var}(\bar{X}_z) \approx S/n_z\), this is the\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morganRerandomization2012}{4}} statistic extended to \(k\) arms.
Under complete randomization it is approximately
\(\chi^2_{p(k-1)}\). The threshold is its 0.001 quantile, so
about one draw in 1,000 is accepted.

An early analysis plan also listed minimization
(Pocock-Simon dynamic balancing) as a comparator. We omit
it. Minimization is a sequential-enrollment procedure. That
is, it balances covariates as subjects arrive one at a
time. This paper addresses a different setting, namely a
fully characterized pool available before randomization.
Rerandomization and matched-tuple randomization are both
pool-based. They therefore provide a more direct contrast.
Minimization is not implemented in \texttt{factorialrandom}.

\subsubsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{evaluation-metrics}

For each scenario and method, we compute across
2,000 simulation replicates:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Covariate balance: maximum absolute standardized mean
  difference across all pairwise arm comparisons.
\item
  Bias of the main effect and interaction estimators.
\item
  Empirical relative efficiency: the empirical variance
  under simple randomization (Neyman estimator) divided by
  the empirical variance under the method.
\item
  95\% confidence interval coverage.
\item
  Type I error (null scenarios) and power (non-null
  scenarios): the proportion of replicates rejecting
  \(H_0: \tau = 0\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\).
\end{itemize}

Three estimators are computed. The Neyman and
Lin-adjusted estimators are computed for every design. The
paired estimator is computed for the matched designs only.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Summary of findings}\label{summary-of-findings}

This manuscript studies matched-tuple randomization for
\(2^F\) factorial designs drawn from a characterized pool. It
gives a greedy tuple-formation heuristic, a
pool-oversampling variant, and short finite-population
arguments for the estimator and a conservative variance
estimator. The asymptotic theory is due to
1. The simulation compares five designs
over 72 scenarios with \(k = 4\), at pool ratios 1 and 2. The
Application adds one design point at pool ratio 4.

The main empirical findings are as follows. All estimators
were close to unbiased. The paired estimator with \(t_{M-1}\)
intervals and the Lin-adjusted estimator with HC2 errors
had coverage close to 0.95, including at \(M = 25\). The
unpaired Neyman estimator was conservative under the
restricted designs. Matched-tuple randomization improved
balance and efficiency over simple randomization. Its
efficiency was similar to that of rerandomization with a
calibrated threshold.
Regression adjustment recovered most of the efficiency gain
under every design. In this linear-normal setting, the
added value of matching over a well-analyzed
rerandomized or simple design was therefore small. Matching
may matter more with nonlinear outcome models, many
covariates relative to \(N\), or skewed outcomes. These were
not simulated.

\subsection{Practical considerations}\label{practical-considerations}

\subsubsection{When to use matched-tuple randomization}\label{when-to-use-matched-tuple-randomization}

Matched-tuple randomization is most advantageous when:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  The full pool of subjects is available before randomization
  (as in trial-ready cohorts). This permits global rather
  than sequential matching.
\item
  Strong prognostic covariates are measured at baseline.
\item
  The number of factorial arms \(k\) is not too large, so as
  to avoid excessive degrees-of-freedom loss. The simulation
  and application in this manuscript test only \(k = 4\)
  (\(2^2\) designs). Larger \(k\), for example \(k = 8\) for a
  \(2^3\) design, is expected to be more susceptible to the
  degrees-of-freedom tradeoff discussed below. However, this
  has not been simulated here. Accordingly, no specific
  upper bound on \(k\) is asserted.
\item
  The pool-to-sample ratio \(r\) exceeds 1. This provides
  excess subjects for quality matching. It also changes
  the estimand to the retained-sample effect (Pool
  oversampling). That change must be stated and justified.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The paired variance estimator has \(M - 1\) degrees of
freedom, against \(N - k\) under simple randomization. With
\(t_{M-1}\) intervals its coverage was close to nominal at
\(M = 25\) in our simulation. Smaller \(M\), or larger \(k\) at
fixed \(N\), was not studied.

A second limitation is computational and algorithmic. The
package ships only the greedy heuristic (Tuple formation).
It needs \(O(N^2)\) memory and distance computations, and
\(O(N^2 \log N)\) time with the per-seed sort. It ran at a
pool of 2,000 in the Application. An exact
integer-programming formulation would guarantee a globally
optimal partition. It has not been implemented or
benchmarked here, and we make no claim about its
feasibility at \(N\) in the thousands. We have not measured
how far the greedy partition is from optimal.

The simulation has further limits. Covariates are normal
with AR(1) correlation, outcomes are normal and linear in
\(X\), and effects are constant. AD change scores are often
skewed, and real TRC covariates are mixed and correlated in
other ways. Only \(k = 4\) is simulated.

\subsubsection{Relation to rerandomization}\label{relation-to-rerandomization}

Rerandomization\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morganRerandomization2012}{4}--\citeproc{ref-liRerandomization2KFactorial2020}{6}} and matched-tuple
randomization address the same goal, namely covariate
balance, through different mechanisms. Rerandomization
restricts the allocation space to a subset of all possible
randomizations. Matched-tuple randomization restricts both
the allocation space and the subject grouping. In our
simulation the two gave similar efficiency. Rerandomization
does not exclude anyone, so it has no estimand cost. The
two approaches can be combined. One would form tuples and
then rerandomize within tuples to further balance residual
covariates. We have not studied this combination.

\subsection{Future work}\label{future-work}

Several extensions merit further investigation:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Fractional factorials.} When \(2^F\) is too large, a
  \(2^{F-p}\) fractional factorial reduces \(k\). Matched-tuple
  randomization extends directly with \(k = 2^{F-p}\).
\item
  \textbf{Unequal allocation.} When factorial arms have unequal
  target sizes (e.g., shared placebo arm), the matching
  problem requires tuples of heterogeneous size.
\item
  \textbf{Adaptive enrichment.} Combining matched-tuple
  randomization with biomarker-driven adaptive enrichment
  designs.
\item
  \textbf{Time-to-event outcomes.} Extending the inference
  framework to survival endpoints.
\end{itemize}

  \ref{simulation-study}.

  \texttt{L\textquotesingle{}Ecuyer-CMRG}. Each scenario-method condition is seeded
